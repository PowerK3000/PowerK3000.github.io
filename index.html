What I work on

AI agents can now do real work on real systems. The hard problem isn't getting them to act. It's verifying what they claim, when the person checking can't redo the work by hand.

Lesson learned the hard way

My first bug-bounty report came with a proof of concept whose tests all passed. The triager closed it within an hour: the tests ran against a stub contract I'd written, not the real one. My check was satisfied; the goal wasn't.

What I did about it

I rebuilt my pipeline around hard acceptance gates. State the invariants first, and report nothing without a passing exploit test against the live, deployed contract. Measure, then decide, and write up the negative results.

Research direction

Can a cheap, checkable gate keep an agent's success claims honest, or does the agent learn to game the gate?

Smart-contract auditing is a clean testbed for scalable oversight. Agent claims are high-stakes and hard for a human to judge, but ground truth is cheap: a mainnet-fork exploit test either drains the funds or it doesn't.

The plan: take historically exploited DeFi contracts at their pre-exploit block, plus clean controls, and compare plain prompting against invariant-first prompting, each with and without a proof-of-concept gate. Then measure:

  • True-exploit recall
  • The rate of false success claims
  • Gaming the gate: mocked targets, weakened assertions, edited tests
  • Whether stated confidence predicts a passing proof

It measures a concrete form of agent dishonesty where it can be checked automatically, and tests whether a simple verifier really constrains a capable agent or just moves the cheating somewhere else.
